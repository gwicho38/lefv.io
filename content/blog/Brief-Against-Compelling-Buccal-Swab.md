*An abstract of a brief I wrote for law school a while back. The full version is linked at the bottom.*

The facts are narrow. In January 2012 a joint FBI–NYPD operation executed an arrest warrant for a murder suspect. On entry the officers were met by a third party who, on the government's account, impeded them. She was charged under 18 U.S.C. § 111(a), and the question became whether that charge lets the government compel a DNA sample from her before any conviction.

The brief argues it does, on two independent grounds.

The first is statutory. DNA collection under 42 U.S.C. § 14135a requires a qualifying offense, and one category of qualifying offense is a crime of violence under 18 U.S.C. § 16. Section 111(a) qualifies on the Second Circuit's categorical approach, which asks whether the offense by its nature carries a substantial risk that force will be used. It qualifies again on a natural reading, because "forcibly" modifies every verb in the statute, and the Second Circuit has held that the modifier narrows what the unmodified verbs would otherwise reach. It qualifies a third time under the modified categorical approach. And where any ambiguity survives, the Justice Department's own regulations name § 111 as a qualifying offense outright, which under *Chevron* resolves it.

The second is constitutional. Collecting DNA from pre-conviction arrestees does not violate the Fourth Amendment under the special needs test: an arrestee's privacy interest in their DNA is minimal, a buccal swab is about as small an intrusion as a search can be, and the government's interest in accurate identification at the time of arrest is substantial.

The through-line is that DNA identification is more protective of privacy than the collection methods it replaces, not less — which is the part that tends to get lost.
