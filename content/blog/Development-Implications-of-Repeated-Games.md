*An abstract of my law school "A-paper." The full version is linked at the bottom.*

Development theory disagrees about almost everything — imperialism and core-periphery economics, property rights and work ethic, institutions. But one theme runs through all of it: social structures matter to growth independent of resources and population, and where individuals lack positive incentive structures, wellbeing suffers. So perhaps these theories can all be valid at once, provided they emphasize whatever pushes a society back toward better incentives.

I used the prisoner's dilemma to test that. Played once, defection dominates; played repeatedly among people who expect to meet again, cooperation becomes rational, because the group can punish defection in future rounds. That gives a way to compare societies: how well does each one suppress an individual's incentive to defect? I applied the question to hunter-gathering, agrarian, and industrial structures.

One feature held constant throughout. Social growth and complexity *decrease* a group's ability to exert the pressure a cooperative equilibrium requires. Hunter-gathering societies come out ahead — small, members expect to meet indefinitely, defection is observable, punishment is immediate and internalized. Complexity erodes every one of those conditions, so complex societies substitute: laws, civil society, theology, and historically imperialism and colonialism. Each is an attempt to manufacture what smaller societies got for free.

Solidarity lending in microfinance is the case that fits. Lending to a jointly accountable group reintroduces observability and peer punishment into a credit market that has neither, and the effect is not subtle: where an individual borrower's average outstanding balance was $1,024, the average balance outstanding to solidarity lenders was $109. The benefits spill past the borrowers, with one World Bank study finding social capital and economic empowerment increased equally for participants and non-participants in program areas.

This supports the institutionalist position, and suggests why it holds — institutions are the machinery a complex society needs to produce cooperative pressure a small one generates on its own. Which leaves the question the paper ends on: can reverting to our past social structures help shape and grow our future?
