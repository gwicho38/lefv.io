Development theory disagrees about almost everything. Some scholars attribute the disparity in wealth to imperialism and core-periphery economics. Others point to property rights, religion, and work ethic. Institutionalists argue that organizations, rules, and outcomes combine into institutions, and that institutions are what make growth sustainable.

One theme runs through all of them: social structures matter to economic growth, independent of resources and population. And where individuals lack positive incentive structures, socioeconomic wellbeing suffers.

So perhaps these theories can all be valid at once, provided they emphasize the factors that push a society back toward better incentive structures. I wanted a way to test that idea, so I used the prisoner's dilemma as a model.

## The model

The prisoner's dilemma is the cleanest available statement of the tension between what is good for an individual and what is good for the group. Played once, defection dominates. Played repeatedly among people who expect to meet again, cooperation becomes rational, because the group can punish defection across future rounds.

That gives a way to compare societies: how effectively does each one minimize an individual's incentive to deviate from the cooperative equilibrium?

I applied that question to three prototypical social structures — hunter-gathering, agrarian, and industrial — across their standards of living, social welfare, social structures, and political structures.

## What the comparison shows

One feature held constant across the entire analysis: **social growth and complexity decrease the ability of a group to exert the pressure needed to reach a cooperative equilibrium.**

Hunter-gathering societies come out ahead. They are small, members expect to meet each other indefinitely, defection is observable, and punishment is immediate and internalized. The conditions that make repeated play produce cooperation are simply present by default.

As societies grow more complex, those conditions erode. Encounters become anonymous and one-shot. Observation gets harder. Punishment stops being automatic. So complex societies substitute: laws, civil society groups, theology — and, historically, imperialism and colonialism. These are all attempts to replace something that smaller societies got for free.

## Solidarity lending

If the model is right, development schemes that reconstruct small-group incentive conditions should outperform ones that don't. Solidarity lending in microfinance is a good test, because it does exactly that: it lends to a group whose members are jointly accountable, which reintroduces observability and peer punishment into a credit market that otherwise has neither.

The results line up. Solidarity lending lowers the credit monitoring costs of assessing, managing, and collecting loans, and it lowers default risk for the individual and the group. The magnitude is not subtle — where an individual borrower's average outstanding balance was $1,024, the average balance outstanding to solidarity lenders was $109.

The benefits also spill past the borrowers. One World Bank study found social capital and economic empowerment increased equally for participants and non-participants in program areas. Another found a positive correlation between repayment and group involvement. Microfinance has also been found to cure information asymmetries in rural loan markets.

There is a gender effect as well. Many women in these societies could not borrow under a standard lending model, and so had difficulty contributing outside the home. Contributing financial resources confers legitimacy, and clients across institutions report receiving more respect from their families and communities — particularly from male members — than before joining.

## Why this matters for policy

The conclusion supports the institutionalist position that institutions matter, and suggests a reason why: institutions are the machinery a complex society needs in order to manufacture the cooperative pressure a small society produces on its own.

Social structures that can punish individuals effectively, and that minimize any particular individual's incentive to defect, are structured most like hunter-gathering societies. The societies whose norms best create internalized punishment regimes are generally the ones that prosper.

Which leaves the question I ended the paper on: can reverting to our past social structures help shape and grow our future?
